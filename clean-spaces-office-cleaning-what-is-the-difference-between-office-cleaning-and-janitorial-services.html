What is the difference between office cleaning and janitorial services?

Office cleaning describes cleaning performed in an office setting, while janitorial services usually describes a recurring program that maintains a property over time. Providers may use the terms differently, so the written tasks and frequency matter more than the service label.

Compare scope rather than names

Both terms can include restrooms, bins, floors and accessible surfaces. One provider may call a recurring office program office cleaning, while another calls the same work janitorial service. A proposal should identify rooms, tasks and visit frequency so you can compare equivalent work. A broader sounding title does not automatically mean a more complete agreement.

Separate routine work from specialty projects

Periodic carpet cleaning, floor finish renewal and detailed window work may sit outside the recurring program. They are still cleaning services, but they have different access and scheduling needs. Identify them explicitly instead of assuming a janitorial label includes every possible task. This distinction helps prevent missed expectations when a property needs work beyond its regular visits.

Choose the arrangement that fits the building

A small office may need a limited recurring scope, while a larger building may require coordinated service across several shared areas. The useful decision is how to keep those spaces ready for their users. Ask the provider to explain what a normal visit covers and how additional needs are requested. That is more informative than debating which term sounds more professional.

Compare proposals on equivalent commitments

Create a clear comparison using the rooms, recurring tasks and service periods each provider describes. A proposal with a janitorial heading may exclude interior glass, while one labeled office cleaning may include it. Names alone cannot reveal those differences. Ask for clarification wherever a term could represent several different scopes.

Keep periodic projects distinct so you can see whether they are included, arranged on another schedule or outside the provider's capabilities. The objective is an understandable service program, not the broadest label. Clear boundaries make it easier to review performance once the agreement begins.

Apply the service to your actual office

An office program may involve a lobby, meeting rooms, private workspaces and shared restrooms, each with different access and use. Identify which spaces belong to the agreement and which information must remain confidential. If the building has several tenants, a building manager’s approval may not answer every suite access question. Discuss the authorized contact and any restrictions before service starts. When occupancy changes, revisit the arrangement rather than expect an old agreement to cover a different building use. This connects the service decision to the people and spaces involved, while keeping sensitive records, alarm details and internal business procedures out of public content.

A practical example

If two proposals describe identical restroom, floor and bin tasks at the same frequency, one may be labeled office cleaning and the other janitorial service. Compare their written commitments, access arrangements and communication rather than the heading alone.
