How do you handle keys, fobs, and alarm codes?

Keys, fobs and alarm codes should be shared only through an agreed access arrangement with authorized cleaning personnel. The property and provider should confirm who may enter, which areas are permitted and how access is returned or changed when service ends.

Limit access to the work required

A cleaner does not need unrestricted access simply because service takes place after hours. Identify the rooms included, the doors used and any spaces that remain off limits. Separate instructions for shared areas from instructions for confidential offices. The goal is to make authorized entry dependable while avoiding unnecessary access to records, equipment or tenant spaces.

Protect access information

Do not place alarm details in a public article, a visible note or a broadly shared message. Use the communication method agreed with the provider and your security contact. Ask how the company confirms which people are authorized for the assignment. Access arrangements should also address the situation in which a fob fails or an alarm does not behave as expected.

Plan for changes and service completion

Staffing and tenancy can change. Explain who must be notified before another person enters and who can authorize replacement access. When the agreement ends, confirm return of physical items and coordinate changes to electronic permissions with the property team. A clear handoff prevents old access from remaining active simply because nobody owned the final step.

Treat access changes as a property decision

A new tenant, a changed alarm arrangement or a replaced lock can affect a cleaning visit even if the written tasks remain unchanged. Keep the authorized property contact involved so the provider receives current instructions. A crew should not be expected to bypass a locked door or seek access information from an unrelated occupant.

When an item is lost or access may have been compromised, notify the responsible property or security contact promptly and follow the building's established process. The cleaning provider can cooperate with that response, but it should not decide independently how the building's security permissions are changed.

Apply the service to your actual office

An office program may involve a lobby, meeting rooms, private workspaces and shared restrooms, each with different access and use. Identify which spaces belong to the agreement and which information must remain confidential. If the building has several tenants, a building manager’s approval may not answer every suite access question. Discuss the authorized contact and any restrictions before service starts. When occupancy changes, revisit the arrangement rather than expect an old agreement to cover a different building use. This connects the service decision to the people and spaces involved, while keeping sensitive records, alarm details and internal business procedures out of public content.

A practical example

For a shared office building, a cleaning fob may need access to the lobby and restrooms without access to every tenant suite. That distinction belongs in the service arrangement, while the actual codes stay private.
