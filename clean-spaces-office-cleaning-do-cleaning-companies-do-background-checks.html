Do cleaning companies do background checks?

Some cleaning companies conduct background checks, but screening practices vary by provider and assignment. Before granting access to an office, ask what screening is performed, which personnel it covers and how the company manages replacements and subcontractors.

Ask what screened actually means

The phrase background checked does not identify the scope, timing or personnel covered. Ask the provider to describe its screening policy at a level appropriate for a customer. You generally need confirmation of the process rather than private employee records. A broad marketing promise should not substitute for an explanation of how the company decides who may work at your property.

Connect screening to access controls

Screening is only one part of protecting an occupied office. Restricted rooms, keys, alarm information and authorized work areas still need clear limits. Consider whether the crew will work with staff present or enter after everyone leaves. Your own access requirements should be shared before service begins so the provider can confirm whether it can meet them.

Include replacement personnel

A familiar crew member can be absent, and a building may require temporary coverage. Ask whether replacement personnel meet the same agreed requirements and how changes are communicated. If subcontractors are used, understand who verifies their eligibility and supervises their work. Consistency in these arrangements matters more than treating the first scheduled crew as the only people who will ever enter.

Screening does not replace site boundaries

Even when a company confirms screening, confidential documents should remain secured and restricted areas should remain clearly identified. Screening describes a personnel process; it does not determine which tenant room a worker is authorized to enter. Ask the provider to confirm how your requirements apply to everyone assigned to the property, including temporary coverage.

Avoid requesting sensitive personal records merely to prove that a policy exists. A customer level confirmation can explain the arrangement without revealing an individual worker's private history. If your organization has a formal vendor access requirement, share it before contracting so the company can determine whether it can meet that requirement.

Apply the service to your actual office

An office program may involve a lobby, meeting rooms, private workspaces and shared restrooms, each with different access and use. Identify which spaces belong to the agreement and which information must remain confidential. If the building has several tenants, a building manager’s approval may not answer every suite access question. Discuss the authorized contact and any restrictions before service starts. When occupancy changes, revisit the arrangement rather than expect an old agreement to cover a different building use. This connects the service decision to the people and spaces involved, while keeping sensitive records, alarm details and internal business procedures out of public content.

A practical example

For an office storing confidential records, the practical issue is who may enter the records room and whether substitute personnel receive the same access restrictions. Screening does not mean every room must be accessible to the cleaning team.
