Do you use employees or subcontractors?

Cleaning companies may use employees, subcontractors or a combination, so the staffing arrangement should be confirmed with the provider. What matters to the customer is who performs the work, who supervises it and which company is accountable if a concern occurs.

Understand who is responsible

The staffing label alone does not tell you whether cleaning will be dependable. A company using employees can still have weak supervision, while an arrangement involving subcontractors can have clear accountability. Ask who holds the service agreement and who handles concerns, access instructions and corrections. The customer should not need to track down an unfamiliar worker to resolve a problem.

Check consistency across the arrangement

If another company will enter your office, confirm how its work fits the agreement. Relevant documentation, screening requirements and property instructions should not disappear when the provider delegates an assignment. Ask whether subcontracting is permitted under your building rules and whether changes require notice. Keep the discussion focused on customer protections rather than assumptions about which employment model is always better.

Plan for communication and coverage

The useful test is what happens when the regular worker is absent or an area is missed. There should be an identified contact who can coordinate a response and understand your scope. Confirm how personnel changes affect access authorization and site familiarity. Do not assume that a staffing arrangement guarantees the same individual at every visit.

Avoid assumptions about staffing quality

Employee status does not automatically mean a person has been trained for your building, and subcontractor status does not automatically mean a person lacks supervision. Evaluate the actual responsibilities and communication chain. The relevant concern is whether the contracting provider remains accountable for the promised work and the people it authorizes to enter.

Ask how changes are communicated and whether the property will interact with one responsible company or several. A tenant should not receive conflicting instructions from an unfamiliar organization. If your building rules restrict delegation, that requirement should be understood before service begins rather than raised after a replacement crew arrives.

Apply the service to your actual office

An office program may involve a lobby, meeting rooms, private workspaces and shared restrooms, each with different access and use. Identify which spaces belong to the agreement and which information must remain confidential. If the building has several tenants, a building manager’s approval may not answer every suite access question. Discuss the authorized contact and any restrictions before service starts. When occupancy changes, revisit the arrangement rather than expect an old agreement to cover a different building use. This connects the service decision to the people and spaces involved, while keeping sensitive records, alarm details and internal business procedures out of public content.

A practical example

A property manager with several office tenants should know which company is authorized to enter and which contact owns the service issue. That remains important regardless of whether the person holding the mop is an employee or a subcontractor.
