Do you use green cleaning products?

Green cleaning products can be requested for an office cleaning program, but the specific products and their suitability should be confirmed with the provider. Look for credible product information and recognized certifications rather than assuming that natural wording or a green label proves a product is appropriate.

Define your building priorities

Green cleaning can refer to ingredients, fragrance preferences, packaging or a broader purchasing policy. Explain the result your building needs instead of relying on a vague label. An office with fragrance sensitivities may have different requirements from a property following a formal environmental purchasing standard. Ask which products will be used on which surfaces and whether alternatives are available for restricted areas.

Distinguish certification from advertising

EPA Safer Choice offers a searchable list of products meeting its standard. That provides a more concrete reference than a bottle described only as eco friendly. Certification still does not mean a product is suitable for every finish or that it serves every cleaning purpose. Review the intended use and manufacturer directions alongside the environmental claim.

Keep performance and compatibility in view

A cleaning program needs to remove the soil present without creating avoidable problems for occupants or surfaces. Disinfection requirements, where applicable, also need the appropriate product and use conditions. Share finish care instructions and fragrance restrictions before service starts. Green purchasing should be connected to the actual job rather than treated as a promise that one product will handle everything.

Make an environmental preference verifiable

A building can request a product list and identify the reasons for its preferences, such as avoiding added fragrance or following a purchasing standard. Confirm the actual product rather than relying on a color on the container. Different cleaners serve different purposes, and a surface restriction can limit which option is suitable.

If a product is claimed to hold a certification, check that exact product in the issuing program's records. A manufacturer's certification for one item does not establish that every product in its range qualifies. Also ask how requested changes will be communicated so an alternative does not silently replace the approved selection.

Apply the service to your actual office

An office program may involve a lobby, meeting rooms, private workspaces and shared restrooms, each with different access and use. Identify which spaces belong to the agreement and which information must remain confidential. If the building has several tenants, a building manager’s approval may not answer every suite access question. Discuss the authorized contact and any restrictions before service starts. When occupancy changes, revisit the arrangement rather than expect an old agreement to cover a different building use. This connects the service decision to the people and spaces involved, while keeping sensitive records, alarm details and internal business procedures out of public content.

A practical example

An office that requests fragrance free cleaning should confirm the actual product selection rather than assuming every product sold as green has no fragrance. The agreement can record that preference without disclosing chemical formulas or purchasing strategy.
